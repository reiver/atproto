ATProto Managing App

the service that decides who may read and write a “managed” space

by Charles Iliya Krempeaux

A managing app is a type of AT-Protocol (ATProto) service that decides who may read, and who may write, a permissioned space.

The proposal that defines it puts it this way:

com.atproto.managedspace.policy delegates access decisions to an external managing app. This enables dynamic policies (e.g. follower-gating or paid subscriptions) without the PDS maintaining a materialized member list.

A managing app does not store records, does not serve records, and is not called by users or by applications that want to read the space. It is called by exactly one thing — the space host — and it answers exactly one question: “may this account read, and may it write?”

Think of it as the space host's outside advisor. The space host still does all the work (issuing space credentials, keeping the writer set, sending notifications). It just asks the managing app before it says yes.

Note! At the time of writing, spaces and managing apps are a proposal — proposal 0016, “Atproto Spaces”. They are not (yet) part of the ATProto specification. The proposal itself says:

This is a proposal, not the final specification. Details, terminology, and behaviors are all likely to change.

This article describes the proposal as of November 2026. And, the proposal describes the managing app in only a few paragraphs — so, some details are not yet decided. (See “What the Proposal Leaves Open”, below.)

Spaces and Policies, Briefly

A space is a group of records that only some people (and some applications) are allowed to read — for example: a private forum, a paid newsletter, or a user's bookmarks.

Why Have a Managing App?

A member list works well when the members do not change much — for example, a private forum where someone adds and removes people by hand.

But, some rules are not really lists. For example:

  • “people I follow may read this”,
  • “people who have paid for this month may read this”.

With a member list, someone would have to keep updating the list every time someone followed, unfollowed, paid, or stopped paying. (The proposal calls this a “materialized member list”.)

With a managing app, nobody keeps a list on the PDS. Instead, each time the space host needs to know, it asks the managing app — and the managing app works out the answer however it wants (by checking a follow graph, a payment system, a database, etc).

Who would run a managing app? Whoever owns the rule — for example, a newsletter service that knows who has paid. That might be the same application that also runs a syncer for the space (to show the newsletter to its readers). Nothing in the proposal says they have to be different. But, they are different roles.

Setting Up a Managed Space

A space is created by an application calling com.atproto.space.createSpace on the user's PDS (which needs an OAuth scope with manage=create). The user who creates it becomes the space authority. An existing space can be switched to this policy with com.atproto.space.updateSpace (which needs manage=update).

Both take the full policy object. For a managed space, it looks something like this:

managingApp

required. Which service to ask. It is a did#serviceId reference — a DID, a #, and the id of a service in that DID's DID document.

publishPolicy

optional (default false). If true, the PDS publishes this policy object as a record (collection com.atproto.space.policy, record key self) in the space authority's own space repo. That way readers (and syncers) can see which managing app the space uses.

(The did:web:access.example.com#managing_app value above is made up for this article. The proposal does not say what the service's id or type should be.)

The One End-Point: checkAccess

A managing app implements one XRPC API end-point — checkAccess. The proposal describes it like this:

Being a “query” means it is an HTTP GET, with its inputs in the query string.

What Goes In

The proposal says the space host passes 3 things. (The parameter names come from the draft lexicon — see “What the Proposal Leaves Open”, below.)

the space (space)

which space is being asked about — as a space URI (at://…/space/…/…). Required.

the account (repo)

the DID of the account being checked. (The draft lexicon makes it optional.)

the client (clientId)

the attested OAuth client_id of the application, if one was presented. (That is, the application proved who it is with a client attestation.) This lets a managing app make rules like “only my own app may read this”.

What Comes Out

2 answers — read and write. They are independent of each other. The proposal says:

The app answers read and write access as independent values which allows the expression of mixed arrangements, such as a paid newsletter that subscribers may read but only the authority writes to.

Who May Ask

checkAccess is called with service auth from the space authority. Service auth is a short-lived JWT, sent in the Authorization: Bearer header, with (among other things):

iss

who is asking — here, the space authority's DID. The token is signed with the space authority's #atproto key.

aud

who it is for — the managing app.

lxm

which end-point it may be used for — checkAccess.

exp

when it expires.

So, a managing app checks the token by resolving the iss DID, getting its #atproto public-key, and verifying the signature.

One more check is worth doing. The space authority is the DID at the start of the space's at:// URI. So, the iss should match it. Otherwise, any account could ask about anyone else's space — and learn who has access to it. (This check is not spelled out in the proposal. But, the proposal does say the service auth comes from the space authority.)

When the Space Host Asks

The space host needs an access decision at 2 times — when someone wants to read, and when someone has written.

Reads: Issuing a Space Credential

To read a space, an application (a syncer) calls com.atproto.space.authorizeSpaceSync on the space host, to get a space credential. It includes a delegation token (which identifies the user it is acting for), and maybe a client attestation (which identifies the application).

If the managing app answers read: true, the space host issues a space credential. If not, it does not.

Writes: Tracking a Writer

When a user writes a record to a space, the user's PDS (the repo host) calls com.atproto.space.notifyRepoUpdate on the space host. The space host should reject it if the account is not allowed to write.

Notice there is no client here. The proposal says:

A check prompted by a write passes no client, since notifyRepoUpdate does not identify the originating app.

If the managing app answers write: true, the space host adds the account to the writer set, and notifies syncers. If not, the write is not tracked — so syncers will not find out about it.

(Note that answering write: false does not stop someone from putting records in their own space repo. It just means the space does not count them. The proposal says write access “does not prevent an account from writing records into its own space repo”.)

Never: the Space Authority Itself

The space authority is never asked about. The proposal says:

Under every policy, the space authority's own account is always authorized as a user, for both reading and writing, without appearing on a member list or being approved by a managing app.

So, the owner of a managed space cannot be locked out of it by its own managing app.

Taking Access Away

Suppose a subscriber stops paying. What happens?

The managing app just starts answering read: false. But, it only gets asked when the space host needs to know. So:

reads

A space credential that was already issued stays valid until it expires — 10 minutes by default (and at most 60 minutes). After that, the next authorizeSpaceSync gets a no. (If that is not fast enough, the space host may also revoke credentials early with com.atproto.space.notifyCredentialsRevoked.)

writes

An account that is already in the writer set is not removed just because the managing app changed its mind. The proposal says:

Writers approved by the managing app can be explicitly removed from the writer set with com.atproto.space.removeWriter.

And, removeWriter is called on the space host by an app acting for the space authority, with an OAuth scope that has manage=update. So, whatever runs the rule also needs a way to have that called.

What a Managing App Does Not Do

A managing app decides who may sync the space, and whose writes count. It does not decide what each application shows to each of its users.

An application that syncs a space might serve many users. It has to decide, by itself, which of them may see the space. The proposal gives exactly this example:

Or a space may grant access to accounts that the space authority follows which requires gating actual space access via a managedspace. In either case, the syncing application is responsible for understanding and enforcing this application semantic in the views that it serves.

So, a follower-gated space needs the rule to be enforced in 2 places — by the managing app (for the space host), and by every application that shows the space (for its own users). (This is one reason to set publishPolicy — so applications can see which managing app, and so which rule, a space uses.)

What the Proposal Leaves Open

The proposal describes the managing app in only a few sentences. But, the proposal also points to draft lexicons, on an implementation branch — bluesky-social/atproto pull request #5187 (open, not merged, at the time of writing). The draft lexicon for checkAccess fills in some of the gaps:

  • the full NSID is com.atproto.managedspace.checkAccess. (The proposal only says checkAccess.)
  • the parameters are space (a space URI, required), repo (a DID, optional), and clientId (the OAuth client ID “of the requesting app, if attested”).
  • the response is {"read": …, "write": …} — 2 required booleans.

Its description says: “Check an account's read and write access to a managed space. Called by the space host, implemented by the space's managing app. Requires service auth.”

These details are still not specified, by either the proposal or the draft lexicons:

  • the service id and type in the DID document. This article uses #managing_app and ManagingApp.
  • whether the aud includes the #serviceId. The example code below accepts both.
  • caching, timeouts, and what happens when the managing app is down. The proposal does not say whether a space host may remember an answer, or what to do if the managing app does not respond.

And, since the lexicons are drafts, expect all of this to change.

A few things to note:

  • It serves its own DID document at /.well-known/did.json, so did:web:access.example.com#managing_app resolves to it. (In real use it must be reachable over HTTPS at that hostname.)
  • It refuses requests without a valid service auth token (401), and requests from anyone other than the space's own space authority (403).
  • It ignores client. A real managing app could use it to only allow reading through particular applications.
  • It does not check jti for replays, and keeps its subscriber list in memory. A real one would check against a payment system (or a follow graph, etc).
  • The NSID, the space and repo parameters, and the response format match the draft lexicon. (It ignores clientId.) The service id/type are this article's guesses. (See “What the Proposal Leaves Open”, above.)

Since there is no space host that supports managedspace to test against (yet), this code was tested with Go's httptest, using service auth tokens signed with test keys. Signed by Alice and asking about Bob, it answered {"read":true,"write":false}. Asking about Carol, it answered {"read":false,"write":false}. Tokens signed by another account, forged tokens, and tokens with the wrong aud or lxm were all refused.

Summary

An ATProto managing app:

  1. only exists for spaces with the com.atproto.managedspace.policy policy,
  2. is named in that policy's managingApp field, as a did#serviceId reference,
  3. implements one end-point, checkAccess, which answers read and write for one account,
  4. is only ever called by the space host, with service auth from the space authority — when issuing a space credential, and when tracking a write,
  5. is never asked about the space authority itself, and
  6. never stores or hands out records.

Again, this is all a proposal. Details are likely to change.
